Everything that makes the daytime sky polarised still happens at night. When the Moon is up, its light is scattered by the same air molecules, and the night sky carries **the same kind of polarisation pattern, centred on the Moon** instead of the Sun.

## A much harder measurement

Moonlight is sunlight reflected by the Moon's surface. Even a full Moon is roughly **400 000 times fainter** than the Sun, so measuring its polarisation needs sensitive instruments and long exposures. Other light gets in the way too: stars, airglow, and light pollution from cities, which has its own polarisation.

Moonlight is also not perfectly unpolarised to begin with. The dusty lunar surface reflects light with a small polarisation that changes with the Moon's phase, so the starting point is different from sunlight.

## What we learn

Comparing the night-time pattern with models of scattering tells us:

- how much **multiple scattering** and how many **aerosols** there are in the atmosphere,
- how artificial light changes the natural polarisation of the night sky,
- how well simple models like Rayleigh scattering work in very faint light.

## Animals that navigate by moonlight

This isn't only a physicist's curiosity. Some nocturnal dung beetles roll their balls of dung in a straight line by following the polarisation pattern of moonlight. On moonless nights they lose their way more easily.
