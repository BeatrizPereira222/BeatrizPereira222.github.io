Put on polarised sunglasses on a clear day, look at the sky about 90° away from the Sun, and tilt your head. The sky gets darker and brighter as you turn. You are seeing **sky polarisation**, a pattern spread across the whole sky that is invisible to the naked eye.

## Where it comes from

Sunlight is unpolarised: its electric field points in random, ever-changing directions. When it hits the molecules of the air, they scatter it in every direction, a process called **Rayleigh scattering**. But they don't scatter every direction of the field equally.

Light scattered at a right angle can only keep the part of its field that is perpendicular to the plane containing the Sun, the molecule and you. So **light from the sky 90° away from the Sun is strongly polarised**, while light from close to the Sun, or from the opposite point, is not.

## A pattern that moves with the Sun

Because the pattern is centred on the Sun, it sweeps across the sky during the day. At sunrise and sunset the band of strongest polarisation runs straight overhead, from north to south. **Try it in the [Sky Lab](index.html#lab)**: set the Sun's elevation to 0° and watch the band pass through the zenith.

## The real sky is messier

The simple theory predicts 100% polarisation at 90°. The real sky rarely gets above about 75–80%, because:

- light is often scattered **more than once** before it reaches us,
- **aerosols** (dust, smoke, sea salt) scatter differently from molecules,
- the **ground** reflects light back up.

These effects also create **neutral points**, small regions where the polarisation vanishes, named after Arago, Babinet and Brewster, who found them in the 19th century. Measuring exactly how the real sky departs from the simple model tells us about the atmosphere itself, and that's what my research is about.

## Who uses it?

Many insects, including bees and ants, see polarised light and use the sky pattern as a compass, even when the Sun is hidden behind a cloud. Some historians have suggested that Vikings may have done something similar with crystals, though that idea is still debated.
