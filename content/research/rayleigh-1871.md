In 1871 John William Strutt, later Lord Rayleigh, was 28. In a paper of about a dozen pages he answered two questions that had puzzled scientists for decades: **why is the sky blue, and why is its light polarised?**

## The question

By the 1860s it was known that skylight is strongly polarised at 90° from the Sun, and John Tyndall had shown in the laboratory that very fine particles scatter blue light more than red. What was missing was a physical explanation.

## The idea

Rayleigh imagined particles **much smaller than the wavelength of light**. The light wave makes each particle vibrate, and the vibrating particle sends out light of its own. Using a dimensional argument, he showed that the scattered intensity must depend on the wavelength as

$$I \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^4}.$$

Short (blue) wavelengths are scattered far more than long (red) ones. The same model predicts that light scattered at a right angle to the incoming beam is **completely polarised**, matching the observations.

## Why it matters

- It gave the first quantitative explanation of the sky's colour.
- The $\lambda^{-4}$ law, now called **Rayleigh scattering**, is used everywhere from atmospheric science to optical fibres.
- Rayleigh later showed that the molecules of the air themselves are enough to do the scattering; no dust is needed.

## What it doesn't cover

The model assumes single scattering by particles much smaller than $\lambda$. Larger particles like water droplets need Mie theory (1908), and the real sky's lower polarisation and neutral points need multiple-scattering models. Those are the corrections my own research works with.
